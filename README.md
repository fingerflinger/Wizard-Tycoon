# Wizard-Tycoon
# San Francisco Cult — Game Design One-Pager

**Genre:** Strategy / faction management  
**Setting:** San Francisco Peninsula, an occult underworld competing for control of the city  
**Inspirations:** *Star Wars: Rebellion*, *Civilization IV*, *Sid Meier’s Alpha Centauri*

## High Concept

Build an occult faction from a small cult into a force capable of transforming reality. Recruit followers, infiltrate neighborhoods, compete with rival factions, and accumulate enough control over the populace to enact a grand scheme. Ultimately, transcend ordinary faction management and compete with rivals at a higher level of power.

## Core Gameplay Loop

1. **Recruit:** Grow your organization from its home base, expanding your pool of agents and followers.
2. **Deploy:** Assign members to missions across San Francisco’s neighborhoods.
3. **Compete:** Recruit followers, gather intelligence, generate resources, and influence local populations while contending with rival factions.
4. **Expand:** Increase your faction’s influence and control over territories. Diplomacy can produce positive, neutral, or negative outcomes.
5. **Execute your grand scheme:** Once sufficient domination of the populace has been achieved, expend that accumulated power to advance the next stage of your plan.

## World & Factions

The city is a strategic map of neighborhoods, each offering opportunities for influence and operations. Competing factions have their own interests and sources of power. Potential factions include Cthulhu cultists, Satanists, vampires, wizards, and technology billionaires.

Missions are the primary means of interacting with the map:

- **Recruitment:** Expand your membership.
- **Diplomacy:** Gain influence and cultivate relationships.
- **Information:** Discover opportunities and rival activity.
- **Economics:** Acquire resources to sustain and expand operations.

## Endgame: Ascension

**The central design goal is to accelerate the game toward a decisive conclusion before late-game micromanagement overwhelms the experience.**

As factions grow, managing numerous agents, territories, and small-scale operations becomes increasingly burdensome. Ascension offers a transition to a more powerful level of play: fewer agents or decision-makers, but decisions with much greater strategic leverage.

- **Trade breadth for leverage.** Ascension sacrifices some direct control over numerous small assets in exchange for powerful actions by an individual or small group.
- **Create a race, not a switch.** The first player to ascend gains an advantage, but the second must retain a viable path to ascension rather than being effectively eliminated.
- **Preserve counterplay.** The transition should create opportunities for the rival to respond, disrupt, or accelerate their own ascension.
- **Reach a conclusion.** Ascension should move the game toward a decisive resolution rather than introduce another layer of indefinitely expanding management.

The intended structure resembles a race toward a civilization-scale victory condition, but with a qualitative change in the player's agency and scale of decision-making.

## Design Pillars

1. **Strategic geography:** Neighborhoods, territory, and local influence matter
2. **Agent missions:** Send agents (characters) on missions, they will develop strengths and the player can development fondness for them. The characters development is a pillar of the narrative, in addition to the changes in the City.
3. **Competing schemes:** Rivals contest both immediate resources and long-term strategic objectives.
4. **Escalating scale:** The game evolves from local cult management toward civilization- or reality-altering decisions.
5. **A fair, decisive endgame:** Early ascension is valuable, but the race remains competitive until the conclusion.

## Key Design Question

What exactly does ascension let the player do, and what control must they surrender in return? The answer should establish a new strategic mode that is more consequential but not simply more powerful at everything.
